AI Scientist Systems

End-to-end autonomous research agents that combine ideation, experimentation, analysis, and manuscript generation.

58 Methodology quality 95 Recency 41 Reproducibility 38 Topical relevance

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

End-to-end autonomous discovery pipelines

Generate ideas, run experiments, analyze results, and produce manuscripts as a full research loop, reducing human labor and per-paper cost.

Open edge: Acceptance rates are inconsistent and validation is often workshop-level or LLM-judged; external human validity is not fully demonstrated.
Domain-specific AI scientist systems with validity gates

Adapt autonomous discovery to applied mathematics and computational fluid dynamics by adding logic verification or physics-aware visual checks.

Open edge: Verification gates can miss planted silent failures; evidence is limited to specific solvers or domains and a small number of generated papers.
Virtual discovery environments and task suites

Provide controllable open-ended tasks for complete discovery cycles and measure task completion, relevant actions, and explanatory knowledge.

Open edge: Strong baselines still struggle, with limited evidence on generalization to unseen tasks or real-world discovery utility.
Evidence-grounded evaluation benchmarks

Hide source conclusions and score evidentiary maturity from data claims using fixed LLM judges and blinded peer-reviewed tasks.

Open edge: Results are based on one frozen base model; agents plateau and lack controls, robustness, falsifiability, and cross-dataset generalization.

Shared Direction

  • Autonomous scientific discovery needs idea generation coupled with executable experiment and analysis loops, not text generation alone.
  • Automated evaluation is necessary for scaling assessment, using LLM judges, task completion metrics, or blinded evidence tasks.
  • Current systems are stronger at producing plausible, well-documented analyses than at establishing trustworthy claims such as controls, robustness, and falsifiability.
  • Generic coding agents lack science-specific failure detection, motivating domain-specific verification gates.

Key Differences

  • Evaluation target differs: generated-manuscript scores, evidence maturity from data claims, or task completion in a virtual discovery environment.
  • Domain scope differs: open-ended ML discovery versus constrained formal mathematics or computational fluid dynamics with injected logic or physics checks.
  • Verification mechanism differs: internal logical coherence review, vision-language inspection of rendered flow fields, or benchmark-based evidence grading.
  • Supervision and interaction mode differs: some systems are framed as human-Principal-Investigator and AI-graduate-student collaboration, while others pursue fully automated discovery.

Open Questions

  • Do evidence-grounded benchmark scores transfer across different base models and scientific domains?
  • Which verification gates catch silent failures outside pre-planted or domain-specific failure modes?
  • Can automated reviewers or LLM judges align with human scientific utility and reproducibility assessment?
  • How do these systems perform in external, uncurated discovery tasks beyond workshop-level or benchmark settings?

Evaluation, Reliability & Governance

Evaluation, reproducibility, safety, epistemic reliability, governance, and benchmark infrastructure for automated research.

59 Methodology quality 95 Recency 41 Reproducibility 36 Topical relevance

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

Autonomous discovery workflow systems

Turning LLM-generated hypotheses into complete loops: code implementation, experiments, visualization, manuscript writing, and simulated or real peer review.

Open edge: Reliability is uneven across submissions and domains; validation often remains single-domain, workshop-level, or dependent on limitation details that are not fully visible in the evidence.
Benchmark and task-environment design

Providing controlled task suites, hidden conclusions, frozen data, metrics, and human baselines for comparable evaluation of discovery agents.

Open edge: Domain and task-format coverage is limited; LLM judges and proxy metrics may not align with expert scientific utility, and generalizability across base models is unverified.
Reliability gates and authority control

Constraining autonomous edits or validating generated results through deterministic program authorities, physics-aware visual gates, and evidence audits.

Open edge: Silent failure detection remains incomplete, such as a vision-language gate missing 2 of 16 planted failures; autonomy-authority tradeoffs across domains are unresolved.
Evidence-maturity and epistemic evaluation

Scoring generated scientific claims along dimensions such as controls, robustness, falsifiability, auditability, novelty, and cross-dataset generalization.

Open edge: Current agents plateau around executing and documenting analyses but lack discriminating evidence practices; only one frozen base model is tested, leaving generalization as an evidence gap.

Shared Direction

  • Automated research should be evaluated as an end-to-end workflow, not only as final model outputs.
  • Current agents can implement and document analyses, but still lack the controls, robustness, and falsifiability needed for trustworthy claims.
  • Automated judging or simulated review is a scalable direction, but its consistency and alignment with expert judgment remain open.
  • Domain-specific reliability mechanisms, such as validity gates or deterministic authority separation, help reduce silent failures and unsafe modifications.

Key Differences

  • Evaluation settings differ: virtual discovery environments with completion metrics versus real domain workflows with physical, biological, or forecasting outcomes.
  • Reliability enforcement differs: deterministic code-edit and promotion control versus vision-language physical verification versus post hoc LLM evidence grading.
  • Success is defined by different standards: peer-review acceptance, benchmark score, evidence maturity, or task-specific performance gain.
  • Generalization claims differ: some systems claim cross-domain ML generality, while others are validated in one physical, biological, or forecasting domain.

Open Questions

  • Do benchmark scores from one frozen base model transfer across other base models and agent frameworks?
  • Can automated validity gates and authority controls detect enough silent failures to support trustworthy autonomous discovery?
  • Can agents move beyond analysis documentation to produce controls, robustness checks, falsifiability tests, and cross-dataset generalization?
  • How well do automated LLM judges and simulated peer review align with human expert judgment outside the narrow tasks reported?
  • Are domain-specific improvements such as physics error reduction or forecasting gains evidence of general discovery ability or task-specific adaptation?
  • Several top papers lack fully visible limitation or artifact details, so reproducibility and failure boundaries remain verification gaps rather than confirmed properties.

Writing & Communication

Automatic paper writing, manuscript drafting, scientific reporting, peer review assistance, and research communication.

51 Methodology quality 95 Recency 40 Reproducibility 34 Topical relevance

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

End-to-end autonomous manuscript generation

Producing full scientific manuscripts from minimal prompts by chaining ideation, code, experiments, visualization, and writing.

Open edge: Reliability is inconsistent; only some submissions pass peer review, and generated conference-ready papers remain difficult.
Verification and evidence-grounding for scientific writing

Reducing unsupported claims and silent failures by auditing logical coherence, inspecting rendered physical fields, or constraining reporting to measured values.

Open edge: Domain-specific gates still miss planted failures, and logical or numeric verification is not shown to generalize across fields.
Human-AI collaboration and oversight in research writing

Placing human intervention at high-leverage decision points rather than requiring full autonomy or exhaustive step-by-step supervision.

Open edge: Which intervention modes transfer across different research domains and team settings is not established.
Evaluation and benchmarking of AI-generated research

Making claims comparable through automated reviewers, peer-review submission, benchmark success such as ARC-Bench, and cost thresholds.

Open edge: Proxy LLM/rubric scores are not yet aligned with broader human peer review, and benchmark coverage remains narrow.

Shared Direction

  • Multi-agent or end-to-end LLM pipelines are treated as the primary mechanism for moving from research ideation to manuscript drafting.
  • Verification or grounding is necessary because raw LLM output can contain hallucinated citations, fabricated numbers, or physically/logically invalid claims.
  • Evaluation should combine quantitative task or benchmark performance with some form of reviewer or rubric assessment of manuscript quality.
  • Autonomous research writing is not yet dependable enough to remove all human judgment, especially for conference-ready scientific claims.

Key Differences

  • Systems choose different autonomy modes: fully autonomous generation and review simulation versus targeted human collaboration at high-leverage decision points.
  • Verification signals differ across lines: internal logical coherence checks versus vision-language physical plausibility gates versus grounded numeric reporting constrained to measured values.
  • Evaluation targets differ: LLM-based rubric scores, workshop peer-review acceptance, or benchmark success such as ARC-Bench are not interchangeable evidence.
  • Domain assumptions differ: some systems target narrow formal or physical domains where validity gates can be defined, while others target general ML writing without domain-specific checks.
  • Failure handling differs: one-shot reflection versus multi-hypothesis causal attribution, leading to different recovery ability and measured improvement.

Open Questions

  • How well do LLM-based review rubrics and simulated reviewers predict human peer-review acceptance, and what evidence would close that gap?
  • Can verification gates be built for domains beyond applied mathematics and computational fluid dynamics without relying on domain-specific visual or symbolic checks?
  • Why do only a minority of fully generated manuscripts pass peer review, and which failure modes arise from writing versus experimentation?
  • What standardized external validation, open datasets, code, metrics, and limitation reporting are needed to compare autonomous research-writing systems?
  • What is the optimal granularity and timing of human intervention for improving manuscript quality without adding exhaustive oversight cost?

Research Lines

Open edge: Evaluation relies heavily on LLM-based rubrics and self-generated outputs; independent external validation and limitation reporting are missing.
Evidence-grounded evaluation and blind task benchmarks

  • Systems are often multi-agent or pipeline-based and couple generation with verification, critique, or evidence auditing.
  • Current agents can execute documented analyses and produce paper-like outputs but are not fully autonomous scientists; human oversight and domain judgment remain necessary.
  • Evaluation is moving toward blind, evidence-grounded, rubric-based, or LLM-judge settings with human baseline comparisons.

  • Success-evidence strength differs: one benchmark reports no statistically reliable pairwise separation among agents, while a framework reports large relative gains without visible limitation evidence.

  • Can literature provenance, citation coverage, and research-gap validity be measured independently from generated paper quality?
  • Do automated rubric scores and discovery benchmarks predict real literature review or research decisions?
  • How do current systems generalize beyond one frozen base model, narrow tasks, and selected domains?
  • Which oversight or verification interfaces reduce recurring failures such as memory degradation and false success declarations?
  • Where are direct comparisons against human literature reviewers and citation-analysis baselines?

Hypothesis & Idea Generation

Systems that propose hypotheses, research ideas, experimental directions, or scientific questions.

57 Methodology quality 94 Recency 40 Reproducibility 37 Topical relevance

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

Closed-loop autonomous research agents with multi-agent ideation and human oversight, e.g., AutoResearchClaw and Claw AI Lab

Turns hypothesis generation into executable experiments with debate, self-healing execution, verifiable reporting, and targeted human intervention at high-leverage decision points.

Open edge: Whether targeted human collaboration generalizes beyond limited case studies and internal AI research topics; the optimal amount and placement of human oversight remain undefined.
Reliability and self-correction mechanisms for research agents, e.g., SAGE and EvoCast

Converts experiment failures into evidence-grounded revisions using structured causal attribution, deterministic routing, and grounded reporting that constrains outputs to measured values.

Open edge: Whether gains reflect scientific insight or local implementation fixes; autonomous writing and conference-ready paper generation remain unresolved.
Domain-grounded ideation with domain-specific verification gates, e.g., AI CFD Scientist

Generates physically or biologically plausible hypotheses by coupling literature-grounded ideation to code execution and domain-specific checks such as vision-language flow-field verification.

Open edge: Verification gates can still miss silent failures; transfer to other domains without rebuilding domain-specific checks is unproven.
Benchmarks and controlled evaluation of AI scientists, e.g., BAISBench, Agent4Sci, and ARC-Bench

Provides tasks, human baselines, and in-the-wild settings to measure hypothesis-driven discovery and expose failure modes such as novelty miscalibration and engineering-only gains.

Open edge: Proxy benchmarks may not predict real scientific utility; coverage is limited to selected domains, topics, and internal case studies.

Shared Direction

  • Grounded execution and domain-specific checks help distinguish defensible hypotheses from unsupported claims.
  • Current AI-scientist systems can support discovery but do not yet achieve fully autonomous scientific research.
  • Human judgment or expert comparison remains important for evaluating novelty, completeness, and safety.
  • Benchmarks and ablations are used to expose failure modes rather than only ranking agents.

Key Differences

  • Supervision mode: targeted human collaboration at high-leverage points versus full autonomy versus step-by-step human oversight.
  • Failure attribution: simple reflection versus structured multi-hypothesis causal diagnosis with routed interventions.
  • Evaluation target: domain-specific metrics such as flow error or forecasting performance versus general research benchmark scores versus expert preference.
  • Verification strategy: vision-language physical checks, verifiable reporting, deterministic source-edit boundaries, or benchmark validity gates.
  • Deployment scope: domain-specific scientific agents versus general-purpose AI research teams.

Open Questions

  • Can current benchmark gains be decomposed into novel research insight versus ML-engineering tricks?
  • How can verification gates be made robust enough to catch silent failures across domains without missing unacceptable cases?
  • Do proxy benchmarks predict real scientific utility or publication-grade discovery?
  • What is the optimal placement and amount of human collaboration across different research phases?
  • Can domain-specific systems transfer to new scientific domains without rebuilding verification and evaluation infrastructure?

Experiment Automation

Agents that design, execute, monitor, analyze, or iterate experiments, including code-based and lab-based experimentation.

63 Methodology quality 95 Recency 41 Reproducibility 41 Topical relevance

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

End-to-end autonomous scientific discovery agents

Automates hypothesis generation, code writing, experiment execution, visualization, and manuscript preparation as a continuous loop.

Open edge: Output quality is inconsistent and often judged by automated rubrics rather than external human peer review; one-in-three workshop acceptance was reported.
Evaluation environments and benchmarks for discovery agents

Provides comparable tasks and metrics for complete discovery cycles, including task completion, task-relevant actions, and explanatory knowledge.

Open edge: Synthetic or ML-benchmark success may not capture real scientific insight; in-the-wild gains can come mostly from ML-engineering tricks.
Failure-aware memory for iterative experimentation

Converts failed attempts into bounded, typed negative knowledge that downstream agents can adopt or reject, improving success while reducing tokens.

Open edge: Evidence is limited to a retry benchmark and PDE tasks; generalization across broader scientific domains and memory types remains unverified.
Automated verification and review feedback loops

Adds internal logical audits, simulated review, or vision-language reviewer feedback to catch errors and improve generated papers or experiments.

Open edge: Automated reviewers may not align with human judgment; optimizing for proxy review could inflate perceived quality without external validation.

Shared Direction

  • Autonomous research systems now cover substantial parts of the scientific loop: ideation, code execution, experiment management, result analysis, and manuscript production.
  • Automated judges and benchmark metrics are the dominant evaluation mechanism, but they are used alongside human or peer-review cases only in limited settings.
  • Persistent memory or feedback from prior attempts improves agent performance and reduces repeated failures, as shown by negative knowledge and verification loops.
  • Current agents often struggle on open-ended discovery or in-the-wild scientific tasks despite strong performance on narrower coding or single-step tasks.
  • Verification and reproducibility artifacts are treated as key components, but the evidence for external validation is still thin.

Key Differences

  • Scope: some systems target full manuscript generation, while others measure task completion in virtual environments or improvement over published benchmarks.
  • Supervision and interaction: designs range from fully autonomous pipelines to human-principal-investigator/agent collaboration and interactive dashboards.
  • Feedback mechanism: systems differ in using internal logical audits, simulated reviewers, vision-language reviewer feedback, or workshop peer review.
  • Evaluation target: synthetic task completion, benchmark score improvement, and peer-review acceptance are not interchangeable proxies for scientific merit.
  • Memory and state: some agents maintain failure-aware shared memory, while others rely on stateless or short-horizon generation and retries.

Open Questions

  • Do gains on synthetic discovery tasks or scientific ML benchmarks transfer to real laboratory experiments and novel domain discoveries?
  • Can automated reviewers align with human peer review without rewarding superficial paper quality or novelty miscalibration?
  • How much of reported improvement is attributable to engineering tricks, better infrastructure, or explicit failure memory rather than research-level insight?
  • What are the conditions under which negative knowledge transfers across tasks and domains, especially beyond retry and PDE settings?
  • Are the platforms and code harnesses externally reproducible across different hardware, datasets, and user settings beyond small internal case studies?

Hypothesis Generation

Papers and resources related to Hypothesis Generation.

66 Methodology quality 96 Recency 39 Reproducibility 46 Topical relevance

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

Autonomous hypothesis-to-result pipelines

Turns an open-ended objective and dataset into iterative data analysis, literature search, hypothesis generation, and experiment execution, producing traceable reports.

Open edge: Long-horizon accuracy and factuality are incomplete; reported statement accuracy is below full reliability, and scaling was tested only up to a bounded number of cycles.
Structured search and evolutionary research trajectories

Explores hypothesis, algorithm, or equation spaces through branching, backtracking, or evolutionary selection, yielding more controlled and inspectable research trajectories.

Open edge: Scaling beyond tested combinatorial optimization, driving, or forecasting cases and isolating search gains from model or task-specific priors remain unresolved.
Human-AI collaboration and oversight protocols

Allocates human intervention to high-leverage decision points to improve reliability without requiring exhaustive step-by-step oversight.

Open edge: Which decision points justify intervention and how autonomy boundaries generalize beyond the reported ablations lack systematic external validation.
Evaluation benchmarks and evidence design

Measures real-world research capabilities with realistic tasks and public evaluation harnesses, quantifying difficulty shifts across model generations.

Open edge: The alignment between benchmark metrics and true discovery novelty, repeatability, or downstream utility is not established, and domain coverage remains limited.

Shared Direction

  • Systems converge on iterative cycles of analysis, literature search, hypothesis generation, and experimental validation, with language agents writing or executing code to test hypotheses.
  • Evidence grounding and traceability are treated as core reliability mechanisms: reports or results should cite code, data, or prior literature, or pass deterministic authority checks.
  • Evaluation relies on benchmark tasks and baseline comparison, but reported accuracy or benchmark performance still leaves residual error or untested edge cases.
  • Autonomy is viewed as domain-conditioned: credible in structured, executable, rapidly verifiable settings, while embodied, delayed, heterogeneous, or ethically accountable settings remain limited.

Key Differences

  • Interaction mode differs: some systems rely on long-horizon fully autonomous operation, while others show that targeted human collaboration at high-leverage points outperforms full autonomy or exhaustive oversight.
  • Exploration mechanism differs: structured tree or evolutionary search uses branching and backtracking to control trajectory, whereas parallel iterative loops rely on large-scale agent rollouts and information sharing.
  • Authority design differs: some separate cognition from deterministic program authority for source edits and promotion, while others depend on agent self-verification and traceable reporting to prevent fabricated outputs.
  • Evaluation target differs: biology research benchmarks, scientific equation discovery, combinatorial optimization, and real-world forecasting cases are not directly comparable because they assess different research capabilities.

Open Questions

  • How do hypothesis quality and factual accuracy change beyond the tested number of cycles or agent rollouts? Existing evidence covers only bounded runs and specific benchmarks.
  • Do proxy research benchmarks such as ARC-Bench and LABBench2 correlate with real scientific discovery value, novelty, reproducibility, or downstream utility?
  • Under what conditions is human intervention necessary, and which decision points produce the largest reliability gain per unit of human effort?
  • Can systems reject weak directions early and avoid fabricated numbers or hallucinated citations in open, non-code-executable research environments?
  • How much of the reported improvement comes from hypothesis generation itself, versus task-specific priors, baselines, or execution and controller design?

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

Agentic iterative survey generation

Automates retrieval, outline expansion, section drafting, and rubric/reviewer-driven refinement to produce organized, long-form literature surveys.

Open edge: Many evaluations use proxy coverage/structure/citation metrics; matched-backend end-to-end comparisons and rigorous cross-domain external validation are missing.
Structured knowledge and taxonomy organization

Replaces flat paper/document views with methods-entity relations or hierarchical taxonomies to improve retrieval, outlining, and topic organization.

Open edge: Semantic relation support is incomplete, with relation types such as extends weakest; alignment to a single expert taxonomy remains low and assumes that reference taxonomy is valid.
Evidence-gated citation and synthesis verification

Uses full-text extraction, citation graph expansion, hybrid filtering, semantic evidence gates, and multi-agent verification to reduce unsupported claims.

Open edge: Audits still report partial or unsupported citations and semantic edges; evidence gates achieve moderate AUC and do not ensure statement-level correctness across all topics.
Evaluation and benchmarking for survey quality

Provides benchmarks, taxonomies, pairwise arenas, and metrics for retrieval, taxonomy structure, content coverage, and citation quality comparisons.

Open edge: Reference-based alignment scores disagree with reference-free capability gaps; benchmarks often depend on single expert-authored taxonomies or narrow proxy metrics.

Shared Direction

  • LLM-based agentic systems are the dominant way to automate survey generation, with retrieval, iterative planning, and multi-stage refinement as core components.
  • Survey quality is evaluated through coverage, structure, citation fidelity, and taxonomy or outline alignment rather than any single surface-level score.
  • Citation reliability and retrieval quality are treated as primary failure modes, prompting evidence gates, citation filters, and paper-level grounding.
  • Structured organization, especially hierarchical taxonomy or method-entity relation modeling, is considered central to producing expert-like surveys.
  • Automated metrics and LLM/human judgment are used together, but results show expert alignment remains difficult.

Key Differences

  • Representation differs: some systems operate over flat retrieved documents while others build methods-entity knowledge graphs or expert-like taxonomies.
  • Evaluation target differs: rubric-aligned quality, pairwise human-preference comparisons, expert taxonomy alignment, and citation precision/recall are optimized by different lines of work.
  • Interaction mode differs: fully autonomous generation pipelines contrast with governed scientist-in-the-loop or aggregate-only research orchestration.
  • Task scope differs: some work targets end-to-end survey drafting, others focus narrowly on taxonomy organization, retrieval evaluation, or research workflow orchestration.
  • Evidence enforcement differs: some systems use semantic evidence gates and citation graph constraints, while others rely on iterative LLM reviewer or rubric feedback without statement-level evidence gating.

Open Questions

  • Why does the best deep research agent retrieve only 20.92% of expert-cited papers, and how much of that is retrieval failure versus legitimate expert curation mismatch?
  • Can methods-entity knowledge graphs improve weak semantic relation types such as extends, which were supported at only 52.8% in human adjudication?
  • Do rubric scores, semantic path similarity, and citation metrics predict actual researcher utility or expert acceptance of a generated survey?
  • How do these systems perform in matched-backend end-to-end comparisons rather than bounded-context concurrent-system comparisons?
  • Can governed end-to-end research systems generalize from a hypertension GWAS case to diverse survey-generation topics while preserving institutional privacy and reproducibility?

Research Agent Benchmarks

Papers and resources related to Research Agent Benchmarks.

30 Methodology quality 95 Recency 40 Reproducibility 17 Topical relevance

Literature review synthesis

Research Lines

Long-horizon research orchestration and state management

Coordinates parallel research workers by moving durable research state into a harness and assigning focused role-specific contexts, reducing context replay and duplicated work.

Open edge: Evidence is reported mainly on software engineering, kernel optimization, and algorithm design tasks; external validation beyond these settings remains a verification gap.
Closed-loop feedback control with surrogate repair

Uses sparse high-fidelity evaluations to update a cheap surrogate during long closed-loop searches, reducing oracle queries and decision regret without per-episode high-fidelity feedback.

Open edge: Generalizability beyond controlled synthetic settings and the MADE benchmark is not established, and fixed surrogate replacement may leave persistent model errors.
Evaluation design and agent-generated feedback benchmarks

Tests whether agents can design executable evaluators under fixed budgets and whether AI-scientist choices survive held-out transfer or hidden sealed targets.

Open edge: Generated evaluators still rank below target agreement ceilings, and the best development-target evaluator is not necessarily best on sealed targets.
Reusable experience and operational skill distillation

Extracts and distills prior experimental trajectories or GitHub repository knowledge into reusable experience or verified skills, improving research performance while reducing interaction cost.

Open edge: Coverage beyond selected repositories and benchmark suites, plus long-horizon accumulation effects across domains, still lacks broad validation.

Shared Direction

  • Long-horizon autonomous research benefits from externalizing persistent research state or reusable knowledge out of individual agent contexts.
  • Fixed budgets and combined metrics such as final performance plus token, oracle, or execution cost help expose efficiency differences that task success alone can miss.
  • Held-out, sealed, or reproduction-based evaluation guards against overfitting to visible development sets.
  • Research agents need explicit orchestration, feedback, and resource-control mechanisms to avoid duplicated work and wasted experimentation.

Key Differences

  • Externalization mechanisms differ: harness-owned search state, accumulated experience libraries, distilled repository skills, and online-repaired surrogates each externalize different granularities of knowledge.
  • Feedback strategies vary from per-episode high-fidelity feedback to sparse acquisition-driven feedback, targeted pilot experiments, and agent-generated evaluators.
  • Evaluation targets differ across final task score, token/oracle efficiency, evaluator pair agreement, held-out transfer, and reproducible task completion.
  • Interaction modes differ among stateless role-specific agents, agent-agnostic coding workbenches, and skill-equipped research agents.

Open Questions

  • Do gains on current research benchmarks transfer to real scientific discovery outside the tested coding, materials, and time-series settings?
  • Can agent-built evaluators close the gap to target-benchmark agreement ceilings without overfitting development targets?
  • Which externalized state or knowledge representation best supports generalization across longer horizons and domains?
  • What failure modes emerge in multi-domain or resource-constrained deployments not covered by the current evidence packet?
